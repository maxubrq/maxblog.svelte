## A line that would not stay still

That evening I opened an application form for a Senior Software Engineer role. It was not a step up, and it was not a shot at a level above mine. It was the level I had been working at for years. And yet when I reached the part where you describe your experience, I stopped for a long time in front of every line.

"Designed and operated a system processing..." I deleted it, typed it again, and deleted it again.

It was not that I had forgotten what I had done. I remembered the nights spent debugging, the arguments about architecture, the times a system held up because of a decision I had made months earlier. But once I had to write it down for a stranger to read, all of it felt strange, as if I were retelling someone else's dream.

What bothered me most was not the lack of confidence. It was that I could not tell where the feeling came from. My ability had certainly not disappeared; I had never stopped working on my craft. So what had I lost?

## First diagnosis: the company took it

My first thought was: of course, a bad company takes its employees' confidence with it when they leave. Or, more precisely, a bad manager does.

The thought is not baseless. Bennett Tepper, in his 2000 study of abusive supervision, followed employees working under managers who belittled them, mocked them and denied their contributions. The consequences he recorded did not stop at job dissatisfaction; they spread into emotional exhaustion, conflict between work and the rest of life, and a lasting drop in how people rated themselves. The research that followed kept making the same point: the effects do not end the day someone quits.

If I stopped here, this would be an essay about blame. I realised I could not stop here, for a very simple reason: if bad managers were the only cause, people leaving good environments ought to walk out fully confident. But I know plenty of people who left good companies and good managers and still sat in front of an application form feeling exactly the way I did.

So there had to be another mechanism, underneath the "bad manager" layer, more common and less often named.

## Confidence is not a feeling, it is a record

In 1977 Albert Bandura proposed the idea of self-efficacy: a person's belief in their ability to carry out a specific task successfully. The important part of his theory is not the definition but the question of what feeds that belief. He identified four sources.

The first is **mastery experience**, having done it yourself. This is the strongest. The second is **vicarious experience**, seeing someone like you do it. The third is **verbal persuasion**, other people telling you that you can. The fourth is your physical and emotional state when you face the task.

Put those four sources next to the life of an engineer inside a company, and something rather stark shows up.

**Mastery experience** only feeds belief when it is recognised as yours. But in a team, output is collectivised. The commits live in the company's repo. The design docs live in the company's Confluence. The postmortem says "the team handled it". The praise lives in the company's Slack. The performance review lives in the company's HR system.

**Verbal persuasion** comes mostly from managers and colleagues, which is to say from the people who stay behind when you leave.

Your emotional state is shaped by the environment every single day.

Three of the four sources that feed professional confidence depend directly on the organisation. When you leave the organisation, you do not lose your ability. You lose the supply. And worse, you lose access to the archive.

<PullQuote>
Professional confidence is not a feeling in your chest. It is a record of evidence, stored somewhere. If it is only stored in the company's systems, you leave it behind when you walk out the door.
</PullQuote>

This is where my story started to turn. I had been looking for the culprit at the wrong layer. The company had not taken my confidence away. I had left all of my evidence inside a system I did not own, and was then surprised that I could not take any of it with me.

I had no evidence of my own ability. Not one file recording the decisions I had made. Not one copy of the recognition I had received. Not one number I had measured myself. All I had was memory. And memory, it turns out, is the worst possible place to store this.

## Memory is not a recording

Frederic Bartlett showed as far back as 1932 that people do not retrieve memories the way you read a file. Every act of remembering is a rebuild, made from whatever fragments survive, with the gaps filled by whatever fits the present. Elizabeth Loftus's later experiments showed that a small change in how a question is worded is enough to shift what people believe they witnessed.

The consequence for this story is direct. When I sat in front of that application form in an anxious state, my brain did not open a neutral archive. It rebuilt my years of work out of the materials of my current state. Anxiety builds an anxious version. The achievements go blurry; the failures come into sharp focus.

Daniel Kahneman adds another layer with his distinction between the **experiencing self** and the **remembering self**, and with the **peak-end rule**: we judge a long period mostly by its emotional peak and by how it ended. If the end of a job was a few months of being worn down by a bad environment, then all the years before it get seen through the lens of those few months.

With no outside evidence to check against, the negative reconstruction has no competition. By default, it wins.

<Sidenote>
To be clear: the peak-end rule is best established for short experiences, such as medical procedures or video clips, and how far it applies to periods lasting years is still debated. I use it as a hint about the mechanism, not as a law.
</Sidenote>

## The senior paradox

One detail made me think hard: the more senior an engineer becomes, the harder their best work is to prove.

A junior can point to the feature they built. A senior spends most of their time on what Tanya Reilly, in her talk *"Being Glue"*, calls **glue work**: reviewing other people's code, unblocking the team, writing documentation, spotting early that a design direction will cause trouble six months out. This work makes the whole system run better, but it rarely leaves a trace with the doer's name on it.

Worse, a good engineer's biggest achievements are often the incidents that never happened. A system designed correctly does not go down. If it does not go down, there is no postmortem. If there is no postmortem, there is no hero story to tell. The best fire prevention is done by someone nobody ever sees putting out a fire.

James C. Scott, in *Seeing Like a State*, uses the idea of **legibility** to describe how large systems can only see what has been made readable to them. A village with no land register is, in the eyes of the state, a blank. A senior engineer's career with no record is, in the eyes of a recruiter, or in the eyes of that engineer, also a blank.

I think this is why many people at senior levels lose confidence more easily on the job market than people at junior levels. Not because they are worse. Because their work is more invisible.

## The objections I have to answer

Before going further, I need to face a few questions I have asked myself.

The first: is this just a more sophisticated way of assigning blame? I think two things need to be kept apart: responsibility and mechanism. The company creates the conditions in which the evidence gets locked in; that is the mechanism. Keeping your own evidence is a personal responsibility, and it is one almost nobody teaches engineers. Both are true. Recognising the mechanism does not excuse anyone from the responsibility. It only tells you where the responsibility lies.

The second: people who are really confident do not need evidence, so needing evidence means the confidence is fake. I disagree, and the reason is **calibration**. The goal is not high confidence but confidence that matches real ability, the honest kind that is hard to shake or knock down. Someone who "does not need evidence" has either internalised their evidence so deeply they no longer see it, or is simply overconfident. Confidence built on evidence is not a weaker kind; it is a more accurate kind, and so it holds up better when tested.

The third is the hardest: with the benefit of hindsight, if I plan to rebuild my confidence through simulation, how is that any different from "fake it till you make it"?

## Simulation is not pretending

This question deserves a careful answer, because popular psychology has a very well-known failure on exactly this point. The 2010 study on *power posing* claimed that standing in a "powerful" posture for a couple of minutes could change your hormones and your behaviour. When Ranehill and colleagues tried to replicate it with a larger sample in 2015, the effects on hormones and on risk-taking did not appear. Confidence manufactured with postures and slogans does not survive contact with data.

But there is another kind of simulation, and it is the foundation of training in fields that are not allowed to get things wrong: aviation and medicine. A pilot does not believe they can handle an engine failure because someone told them they are good. They believe it because they have handled it hundreds of times in a simulator, including the times they got it wrong.

Daryl Bem's self-perception theory, from 1972, offers an explanation of why this works: people infer their attitudes about themselves by watching their own behaviour, much as they would watch someone else's. We do not talk ourselves into things. We show ourselves how we act.

So where is the line between simulation and self-deception? I set one criterion: a simulation only counts if it can fail.

If I sit down, reread material on distributed systems and think "right, I know this", that is reassurance. It cannot fail. If I set myself a design problem at the real difficulty of senior work, put a time limit on it, write the pass criteria before I start, and hand the result to someone who will not go easy on me, that is evidence. Because it can very well come out badly.

What I am doing, more concretely, is working through all four of Bandura's sources rather than just one.

For **mastery experience**: I pick a few problems on a par with what I have done before (sometimes harder), rebuild an old system from memory and redesign it with what I know now, and write a complete design doc as if I had to defend it in front of a panel. Every exercise has criteria, and the result is written down.

For **vicarious experience**: I read public postmortems from large companies, stop before the section on the fix, write down how I would handle it, and only then read on. Every time my thinking matches what they actually did is a data point. Every time it does not is also a data point, sometimes a more valuable one.

For **verbal persuasion**: I message a few former colleagues with one simple question: of the things I did while we worked together, which did they find most valuable? The answers often surprise me, because they remember things I had forgotten, or never thought mattered.

For my physical state: I practise under real pressure. Out loud, against a clock, with someone watching. The goal is not to stop being nervous but to get my body used to thinking clearly while nervous.

This is not pretending. The core of it is real: real ability, put through a real test, with a real chance of failing. The only thing being simulated is the setting.

## From repair to infrastructure

But simulation only fixes this one time. If the root mechanism is evidence stored in the wrong place, the real question is how never to end up here again.

As an engineer, I find the answer familiar to the point of being funny. We never let a system's logs live only in RAM. We design a pipeline: capture, durable storage, aggregation, and we put it somewhere that survives when the service dies. So why does the log of my own career live only in my memory?

In 2019 Julia Evans popularised a very simple idea she called a brag document: a file recording what you have done, updated regularly. I think it needs to be promoted into infrastructure in the full sense.

The capture layer: ten minutes at the end of each week, one to three lines. Not "finished task X", but the decision made, the trade-off weighed, the problem untangled, the person who helped me or whom I helped. Seniors are judged on judgement, and judgement can only be reconstructed if the context is kept.

The storage layer: keep it somewhere I own, not somewhere that employs me. Respect the NDA, but for whatever I am allowed to keep, keep a personal copy.

The aggregation layer: every quarter, compress it into one page. Every year, at least one public artifact: an essay, a talk, a piece of open source. That is the kind of evidence no company can take back.

<Sidenote>
This is a habit I have been practising, including while between jobs and through simulation. It is not finished; I still need more time to live with it and test it, though some of it has worked for me, at least so far. I expect everyone will find their own method, but the criteria should still be: write it down, store it, aggregate it, review it.
</Sidenote>

But if this stopped at the individual, the essay would be missing half. The mechanism I have described is not only the worker's fault. It is also a blind spot in how organisations run.

A good manager does not just praise. They write it down, by name, specifically, somewhere the employee can take with them. Amy Edmondson's work on **psychological safety** shows that a safe environment makes people willing to take on harder work, and harder work is exactly where **mastery experience** is made. I want to propose a measure of managers that almost no company uses: do the people who leave walk out more confident than when they walked in?

At the organisational level, offboarding today is mostly collecting the laptop and locking the accounts. It could just as well hand the person leaving a summary of their contributions. A performance review is an assessment of that very person, and yet they usually do not get a copy. These changes cost next to nothing, but almost no one makes them, because no one considers the confidence of someone who has already left to be their responsibility.

## A problem of the times, not just mine

The more I thought about it, the more this personal story looked like a miniature of several larger shifts.

The wave of layoffs in tech over the past few years pushed a great many people out of their companies in a single morning, with access to every system cut off at once. Along with the laptop, the company took back all the evidence of the engineer's years of work.

Remote and async work makes the work even more invisible. There are no corridors any more, no one who happens to see you sitting next to a colleague all afternoon untangling a problem. The only signal left is what gets written down, and what gets written down lives in the company's tools.

And then there is AI. When a significant share of code and documentation is written together with a model, the question "did I do this, or did the tool?" becomes blurry, first of all to the person doing the work. **Mastery experience**, the strongest source of self-efficacy, is diluted at the root. I think the evidence of this era will have to move from output to judgement: which problems are worth solving, what the model proposed, and what I corrected or rejected, and why. The times you said "no" to a plausible-looking suggestion may be the clearest evidence of senior ability today. And they are almost never recorded.

Finally, there is a paradox in the culture of visibility. People who are good at self-promotion look confident. People who do the work quietly lose their evidence. It can look like those are the only two options. I think there is a third: keeping records not to show off, but to audit yourself. A record written for no reader except your own future self, who will one day sit in front of an application form and need to remember who they are.

## Back to that line

I still have not sent the application I started that evening. But I no longer think the problem is that I have got worse.

Ability does not evaporate when we walk out of a company's door. What evaporates is the layer of evidence we thought belonged to us but was only ever renting space in someone else's system. Confidence collapses along with it simply because it no longer has ground to stand on.

What I am doing now is not talking myself into anything. It is gathering back, piece by piece, the evidence I should have kept from the start, and producing new evidence in tests that can fail. Something with a real core, checked by me.

But this story leaves me with a question I cannot yet answer. If a person's confidence can live inside an organisation's systems, how many other parts of our professional identity (what we think we know, what we think we are good at, who we think we are) are also stored in places we do not own, and will only show themselves on the day we leave?
